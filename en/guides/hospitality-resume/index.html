Hospitality resume: server, kitchen and front desk

Hospitality hires fast and reads fast. Managers want to know in seconds where you’ve worked, how busy it was and whether you can start now. Your resume should make that obvious at a glance.

Updated: November 24, 2026

In this guide
  1. What can’t be missing
  2. How to describe your experience
  3. Examples by role
  4. Certifications and languages
  5. If it’s your first hospitality job

1. What can’t be missing

  • Your phone number right at the top. In hospitality, managers call more than they email.
  • Availability: immediate start, weekends, summer season, split shifts… Put it in your summary.
  • Area or city, and whether you have your own transportation, especially for late shifts.
  • One page, most recent experience first.

2. How to describe your experience

“Server at Bar X” doesn’t say much. The type of venue and how busy it was make the difference: a quiet café is not a 200-cover restaurant.

  • Type of venue: café, casual dining, fine dining, hotel, beach bar, catering, events.
  • Volume: covers per service, tables in your section, rooms in the hotel.
  • Concrete duties and tools (POS systems, hotel PMS, digital ordering).

3. Examples by role

Server or bartender
• Floor and bar service on shifts of up to 150 covers.
• Prepared espresso drinks, mixed drinks and classic cocktails.
• Took orders and payments on the POS; closed out the register.

Line or prep cook
• Mise en place, sides and plating on the cold station.
• Logged temperatures and followed HACCP food safety procedures.
• Received deliveries, rotated stock (FIFO) and kept the kitchen clean.

Front desk agent
• Check-in and check-out at a 120-room 4-star hotel.
• Managed reservations in the PMS and on Booking.com.
• Assisted guests in English, Spanish and French.

4. Certifications and languages

Give them their own Certifications section; in this industry, managers look for it:

  • Food handler or food hygiene certificate (for example ServSafe in the US or Level 2 Food Hygiene in the UK). Many venues require one to work with food.
  • Responsible alcohol service, allergen awareness, HACCP, barista or sommelier courses.
  • First aid.
  • Languages with a real level: in tourist areas, a second language can decide the hire.

In ProCVX: the Certifications section shows each course with its issuer and date in the side column, so it’s visible without taking space from your experience.

5. If it’s your first hospitality job

Attitude and availability count for a lot, but you can also show that you’re good with people and work well under pressure:

  • Customer-facing jobs (retail, camp counselor, events).
  • Volunteering at food banks, festivals or community events.
  • A food handler course: it’s short and shows you’re serious.
